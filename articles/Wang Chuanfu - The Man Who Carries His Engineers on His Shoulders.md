# Wang Chuanfu: The Man Who Carries His Engineers on His Shoulders

> Derived from a passage in *The Soul of the Engineer* (《工程师之魂》), supplemented with public reporting
> Image: `../images/cry.png`

![Illustration](../images/cry.png)

## 1. The Weight of a Tear

A passage in *The Soul of the Engineer* puts it this way: his word carries absolute weight at BYD, yet he treats people with warmth and has hardly ever berated anyone. When something displeases him, he points it out and then solves it on its merits — never lashing out, never reaching for humiliating language to wound someone's dignity. He also has a sentimental side. The president's office recalls the tears he has shed over the years: most of the time, they came from feeling how hard the going had been, how much heartache there was, and how difficult it was to keep at it. If a year's results fell short and the bonus pool shrank, leaving everyone's pay below expectations, he would feel he had failed in his duty as the man at the helm — and seeing certain scenes, he would cry.

Here is a man who oversees close to a million employees and sells more than four million cars a year, and what people remember most is not the grand pronouncements he made at launch events, but the tears of self-reproach he shed because his people earned less than they had expected.

That tear can be read as Wang Chuanfu's entire management philosophy: **he sees the company as a technical community, not a machine for extracting profit.** The first responsibility of the man at the top is to make sure that the people who do the work are paid a decent, dignified return.

## 2. From "Millet and Rifles" to Technical Confidence

In 1995, Wang — who had spent years researching batteries — resigned from the Beijing General Research Institute for Nonferrous Metals and struck out on his own. He borrowed 2.5 million yuan from his cousin, a real-estate developer, and founded BYD in Shenzhen. At the time, Japanese firms controlled more than 95% of the rechargeable-battery market, and an automated production line cost a fortune. His answer was the most basic engineering instinct there is: take the line apart, build yourself whatever you can, and replace costly automation with "manual labor plus fixtures." BYD eventually built its first nickel-battery line for roughly a quarter of the investment a Japanese line required — "we beat their planes and artillery with nothing but millet and rifles."

The 1997 Asian financial crisis handed that low-cost, high-flexibility system a historic opening. Panasonic, Sony, Nokia and Motorola became customers in succession, and by 2008 BYD was the world's largest lithium-battery maker.

When he announced in 2003 that BYD would build cars, the news was met with uproar. His reply was a characteristically brazen Wang Chuanfu line: "A car that costs over a million yuan is, to my eyes, just a pile of steel." Behind that bravado was not recklessness, but a long-standing contempt for what he called Chinese industry's "technology phobia": "If others have it, I dare to build it; if others don't, I dare to imagine it. If you can't solve a problem, it isn't because you lack the ability — it's because you lack the courage."

## 3. Engineers Are an Asset, Not a Cost

At the 2008 annual general meeting, he said something that still reads as a wake-up call today: "The company's greatest value is not on the financial statements. The real assets are the ones the accountants and the reports cannot see — the technologies and business models developed by our engineering team… What you see is how much money the company can make; the real driving force is people — and technology."

History has validated the line again and again:

- At the end of 2018, when the industry was pivoting to ternary (NCM/NCA) lithium batteries, he kept pressing in internal meetings on whether lithium iron phosphate (LFP) could deliver 600 kilometers of range, and proposed arranging the cells "as tightly as blades" — the Blade Battery was born;
- To this day he teaches his executives "physics and chemistry lessons," from anodic oxidation to galvanic cells, purely so that technology is understood and communicated accurately;
- He insists on personally delivering the first class for new graduates — three precepts in six Chinese characters: "Modest. Eager to learn. Ready to endure hardship.";
- By 2025 BYD's R&D workforce had reached 120,000 engineers, and more than 50% of the general managers reporting directly to him were graduates the company had nurtured itself;
- In an internal booklet, the *BYD Basic Guidelines*, he laid it down: a decision-maker "cannot make good decisions by staying in an office" — he must regularly go down into the labs, the workshops and the stores.

"BYD is a rather homespun child: it works honestly and studies conscientiously." Addressing the 2025 annual general meeting, he again defined his company in the self-deprecating idiom of an engineer. No cult of geniuses, no manufactured myths — just a complete system of selection, training, assessment and incentives that coordinates more than a hundred thousand people "as if they were a single person."

## 4. Gentleness: A Rare Form of Leadership

Accounts of Wang Chuanfu in the press are remarkably consistent: even-tempered, economical with words, and happiest talking shop. Employees call him "Lao Wang" in private. He arrives at the office early by car, works there all day, and waits until everyone else has finished eating before going to the canteen — where he sits and eats quietly on his own.

A reporter for *Global People* noticed that even late into the interview, when the executive beside him yawned, Wang's gaze never wavered. His regard for the company's fans — the "Di-fans" — is equally unexpected: "When we're in trouble, they cheer us on and stand by us; when unfair things are said about us online, they argue back with everything they've got; when we do have problems, they don't defend us blindly — they criticize and remind us."

*The Soul of the Engineer* adds the softer grain of his character: he doesn't berate people, doesn't use an incident as a pretext for a wider attack, deals with matters on their merits — and yet will weep with self-reproach because his employees earned less than expected. The greater a man's power, the easier it is to treat people as instruments for hitting targets. He, instead, puts people ahead of the balance sheet. Perhaps that is why BYD has been able to turn cohorts of raw graduates into backbone managers, and keep the master-apprentice tradition alive for more than twenty years — **people will walk a very long way behind a leader who will cry for them.**

## 5. Coda: The Engineer's Soul Is Also an Ordinary Human Soul

From a 2.5-million-yuan loan and a single hand-built production line to 4.6 million sales in 2025 and the title of the world's best-selling electric-vehicle maker, BYD's story is usually summarized as "technology is king, innovation is the foundation." But technology does not grow on its own; it grows in people.

What is truly remarkable about Wang Chuanfu may not be how many startling, hard-edged things he has said, but this: he has always believed that his engineering team is the company's most valuable asset — and he is willing to hurt — and to weep — when his people don't take home what they expected.

A company's soul is, in the end, a human soul. Treat engineers as assets, treat employees as partners, and make yourself the one chiefly accountable — that is Wang Chuanfu, a "technology fanatic" who isn't afraid to weep.
