# The Origin of "Ziwen": A BYD Internal Comic and the Culture of Self-Deprecation

> Compiled from public reporting; sources at the end

## 1. What (or Who) Is "Ziwen"?

"Ziwen" is not a piece of name-gossip and not an internet pun. It is **the protagonist of an internal comic strip published by BYD**. In the past two or so years, new hires at BYD have taken to Maimai, Tieba and Xiaohongshu to crack the same self-deprecating line: "I'm Ziwen."

The short answer: **Ziwen is the cautionary character in a comic issued by BYD's President's Office at the end of May 2024 — and later adopted by employees as a shorthand for "I can't keep grinding, and I can't get out."**

## 2. What the Comic Says

The strip has exactly two characters: **Ziwen** and **Anran**.

The two joined BYD at the same time, twelve years before the strip's final panel, with opposite temperaments:

- **Ziwen**: sloppy on discipline, quick to quit at the first hard thing, with no fight in him; he zones out and plays games during skills training;
- **Anran**: observes the rules, takes meticulous notes in training, and even urges Ziwen to apply himself.

Twelve years later, the outcome is drawn in the bluntest possible terms: **Anran becomes a department manager; Ziwen drifts through years of mediocrity and, two years after quitting, still has no job.**

This is morals-first internal propaganda: the message was written before the story was — play by the rules and endure hardship and you rise; slack and you are flushed out. Its cleverness, or perhaps its undoing, lies in what happened next: employees did not identify with the role model. They cast themselves as the failure.

## 3. Why May 2024?

The comic appeared at the exact moment BYD's management culture was turning a corner:

- **The performance system was five months old.** Employees were graded on a five-tier scale — *excellent, good, meets expectations, needs improvement, unsatisfactory*. The first quarterly ratings landed in April 2024, with roughly 7% falling into the bottom two tiers;
- **A "Competition Culture Month" was running alongside it.** Executives and model employees held forums and speeches to roll out a new corporate value with one core word: **competition**;
- **By then, a slogan had changed one character.** *Bi xue gan bang chao* ("compare, learn, catch up, help, surpass") became *bi xue gan bang sheng* ("…win"). The company did not hide the meaning: *sheng* means survival of the fittest — "a company that performs poorly is unacceptable, and so is an employee who performs poorly";
- **Exit had teeth.** Three consecutive quarters of "needs improvement" automatically triggered an annual "unsatisfactory" rating, which could mean leaving the company — at which point whether you stay or go is put squarely on the table.

In other words, the comic was one cog in a campaign that was tightening the screws. It was taken down not long after publication — which is itself very BYD: an internal propaganda piece does its mobilisation job and disappears, leaving behind a single word that keeps circulating online.

## 4. Why Employees Say "I'm Ziwen" as Self-Deprecation

A comic urging everyone to climb ended up being read as a joke at employees' own expense. The reasons are not complicated.

**First, the newcomers arrived in overwhelming numbers.** Over two and a half years, starting in 2022, BYD hired close to 60,000 engineers — more than in the previous twenty years combined. In 2023 alone it took on 32,000 graduates, 17,000 of them with master's or doctoral degrees. These people have strong résumés, real alternatives, and have seen the pace and the pay at internet companies; being drawn as the guy who slacks off in training hits differently for them.

**Second, performance reviews sharpened the old-versus-new divide.** Veteran employees read "needs improvement" as a rebuke to cocky young people who won't work overtime; newcomers read it as a verdict on long-serving staff whose records are thin. One description of the overhaul was "changing the wheels on a speeding truck" — the system was rewritten during a hiring surge, and everyone was swept into the same new system at once.

**Third, the ladder is getting crowded.** A master's graduate can be rated Grade E straight out of school, while there are only so many section-chief posts — 科长, the rung directly above engineer — to go around; positions that older employees took years of climbing to reach are increasingly going to newcomers from elite "Project 985" universities. Competition culture gave "Ziwen anxiety" a concrete name: **you could be the guy playing games in training — or the guy who can't find a job two years after leaving.**

So "I'm Ziwen" became a safe way to complain. That self-deprecation deflates the weight that four Chinese characters — *you sheng lie tai*, "survival of the fittest" — otherwise place on an individual.

## 5. One Word, One Cultural Gap

Worth noting: BYD is thirty years old and has exported almost no management philosophy. There is no "Alibaba flavor," no "ByteDance way." The one piece of internal language everyone at BYD shares is the competition culture. The company's only employee handbook was printed in 2003 and long ago retired — hires in recent years have never even seen a copy. One executive has acknowledged that BYD long cared about "products, technology and the market" and never set out to distil a spirit, because "that's simply how we've always done things."

Against that backdrop, a comic strip unexpectedly took on the job of exporting BYD's culture — except the message it transmitted was not the one management intended, but the employees' actual feelings.

**The origin of Ziwen is, in the end, a piece of internal propaganda that failed; and the fact that "Ziwen" became a meme shows that the company finally has a word everyone understands.** The difference is that the people who understand it use it to laugh bitterly — not to pledge their dedication.
